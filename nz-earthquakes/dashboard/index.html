Data analysis · GeoNet earthquake catalogue · 2000 to Oct 2026

Three in four New Zealand earthquakes are aftershocks.

GeoNet locates around 20,000 earthquakes a year in and around New Zealand. I modelled 175,000 of them, every magnitude 2.5 or larger since 2000, to find out where they strike, how often the big ones come, and how quickly the shaking dies away. Strip out the aftershocks and the country's background rate is remarkably steady. The catalogue also hides a trap: in 2012 the way magnitudes were measured changed.

Key findings

Mostly aftershocks

Of 27,900 magnitude 3+ quakes since 2012, only 24% are mainshocks. Remove the aftershocks and the yearly count holds at about 400–500. 2016 had 4,816 M3+ quakes but just 424 mainshocks.

Each step up is about 7.6 times rarer

The magnitude–frequency slope (b-value) is 0.88. New Zealand averages about 26 M5+ quakes a year, two M6+, and an M7+ every few years.

Kaikōura faded fast

The M7.8 produced 522 aftershocks of M3.5+ in its first day. Its decay exponent of 1.31 means that a month later, the daily rate was under 2% of day one.

The ruler changed in 2012

Before 2012, GeoNet's old system recorded twice as many M3+ quakes a year, but the same number of M5+. The country didn't get calmer: small quakes were rated about 0.35 magnitude lower after the switch.

How this was built

Data

The GeoNet FDSN event service: every event of M2.5+ since January 2000, fetched a year at a time (busy years in halves, as the service caps requests at 10,000 events). The study region is 34–48°S, 165–180°E. The live panel calls GeoNet's public quake API from your browser.

Model

Loaded, typed and de-duplicated in DuckDB with SQL, then analysed in Python. Declustering uses Gardner–Knopoff windows, which are generous, so some independent quakes count as aftershocks. The b-value is Aki's maximum-likelihood estimate above M3.0 with a 500-sample bootstrap; Omori fits are maximum likelihood from 1.2 hours to the end of each window.

Checks

Rates and the b-value use 2012–2025 only, after the 2012 magnitude change. The b-value is stable (0.88–0.93) for cut-offs from M2.6 to M3.6. The first 1.2 hours after a big quake are left out of aftershock fits, because small aftershocks are missed in the coda of large ones.
